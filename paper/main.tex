\title{Target-Aware Text Features Without Fine-Tuning:\\Cross-Fitted Text Experts for Text-Tabular Learning}

\author{%
  Dima Bshara \\
  Technion -- Israel Institute of Technology \\
}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In tables with free-text columns, the strongest MulTaBench pipeline, Joint TAR, makes text features target-aware by
fine-tuning the text encoder on each task. We show that the encoder does not need to change. Our method keeps
e5-small-v2 frozen and adds a few columns that predict the target from the text: a lexical expert, a neighbour expert,
a probe on the full embedding and probes on the encoder's inner layers. Each is \emph{cross-fitted}: a training
row's feature comes from a model fitted without that row's label. On the 20 text-tabular datasets of MulTaBench, the method
scores higher than Joint TAR with every one of the benchmark's five learners (mean gain \gainMin{} to \gainMax{}
AUROC~/~$R^2$, Wilcoxon $p \le \pMax$; \nHolmPass{} of 5 also survive a Holm correction over the project's \nTests{}
pre-registered claims), and the gain is stable across cross-fitting seeds. Cross-fitting is essential, and the depth at which the target is best read varies from task to task.
It needs no GPU, but does not win on toxicity detection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Real tables often mix numbers and categories with free text, such as a review next to its rating.
MulTaBench~\citep{arazi2026multabench} evaluates learning on such tables with one pipeline: each text column
is embedded by e5-small-v2~\citep{wang2022e5}, reduced to 30 dimensions with PCA, and passed with the structured
columns to a tabular learner. In \emph{Joint Frozen} the encoder is used as published; in \emph{Joint TAR}
(target-aware representations) its top layers are first fine-tuned on the target with LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}. Joint TAR
is the stronger baseline, but it fine-tunes the encoder anew inside every learner's run. We ask whether that
target-awareness requires changing the encoder at all.

Four measurements on the benchmark motivate our design.
(i)~\emph{Over-trust:} the fine-tuned encoder embeds the very rows it was trained on; in a re-implementation of Joint
TAR, its prediction head scored \leakGap{} higher on those rows than on test rows (\leakPos{} of \leakN{} runs on
\leakDatasets{} datasets, $p = \leakP$), which a downstream learner may over-trust.
(ii)~\emph{PCA does not look at the target:} the 30 components keep the directions of largest variance, not of most
target signal (a motivation, not a measured cause of Joint TAR's shortfall; Joint TAR's advantage over Joint Frozen
holds at 15, 30 and 60 components~\citep{arazi2026multabench}).
(iii)~\emph{Only the last layer is read}, although the layer from which the target is best predicted is an inner one
in \depthInner{} of 20 datasets.
(iv)~\emph{Exact tokens get blurred} by a sentence embedding (``Riesling'', ``\$45'').

We propose \emph{Cross-fitted Text Experts}: the encoder stays frozen, and target-awareness enters as a few columns,
each a prediction of the target made from the text by a cheap model fitted without the label of the row it describes.
We contribute the method, which plugs into the benchmark's text step with any learner; a pre-registered evaluation
on the 20 MulTaBench text datasets, where it beats Joint TAR with all five learners; and an analysis of why it works.
Code and results: \url{https://github.com/DimaBsharaa/text-tabular-nlp}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Text-tabular learning.} MulTaBench~\citep{arazi2026multabench} compares five learners on text- and
image-tabular datasets under unimodal, frozen and target-aware (TAR) conditions; TAR follows the representation tuning
of TabSTAR~\citep{arazi2025tabstar}, an end-to-end model that reads the text itself. We improve the modular pipeline
instead. Its learners are gradient-boosted trees
(CatBoost~\citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost}, LightGBM~\citep{ke2017lightgbm}), a parameter-efficient MLP ensemble
(TabM~\citep{gorishniy2024tabm}) and in-context tabular foundation models (TabPFN-v2~\citep{hollmann2025tabpfnv2},
TabPFN-2.5~\citep{grinsztajn2025tabpfn25}).
\textbf{Stacking and cross-fitting.} Using out-of-fold predictions of a model as features is stacked
generalisation~\citep{wolpert1992stacked,breiman1996stacked}; cross-fitting plays the same role for nuisance models in
double machine learning~\citep{chernozhukov2018dml}. Our experts are such first-stage models.
\textbf{Probing.} Different layers of a pretrained transformer encode different properties~\citep{tenney2019bert};
our depth probes choose, per task, where to read a frozen encoder.

\paragraph{Setting.}
For one benchmark fold, let $\mathcal{I}$ be the learner's training rows ($|\mathcal{I}| \le 10{,}000$) and $f$ any
MulTaBench learner with its default settings. MulTaBench encodes each text cell as \texttt{"passage: <column>:
<value>"} with e5-small-v2, mean-pools and $\ell_2$-normalises it into $e \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$, and reduces it to 30
dimensions by PCA fitted on $\mathcal{I}$. Our method replaces only this step (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}); the learner,
its settings, the splits and the metrics are the benchmark's own.

\paragraph{Experts.}
We keep Joint Frozen's features and add columns that each predict $y$ from the text, all from linear or
nearest-neighbour models fitted on $\mathcal{I}$.
The \textbf{lexical expert} concatenates a row's text columns (\texttt{"col: value | \dots"}) and applies TF-IDF over
word 1--2-grams and character 3--5-grams (50k features each, sublinear term frequency) followed by Ridge regression; it
keeps the exact tokens an embedding smooths over.
The \textbf{neighbour expert} averages the labels of the 20 most similar training rows (cosine similarity of the frozen
embeddings, weights $1/d$), per text column and for the mean embedding of all text columns.
The \textbf{final-layer probe} is a Ridge regression on the full 384-dimensional embeddings, so no target information is
lost to the label-blind PCA.
The \textbf{depth probes} mean-pool the token states of each layer $\ell = 1,\dots,12$, standardise them with
statistics of $\mathcal{I}$, and fit a Ridge probe $g_\ell$; the three layers with the best cross-fitted score on
$\mathcal{I}$ are averaged, so the training rows decide at which depth the target is best read.
Experts regress one-hot classes or a rank-Gaussian transform of $y$ and output one column per target (for $C > 10$
classes: the ten most frequent, the maximum and the entropy); each Ridge penalty is chosen on $\mathcal{I}$ by the
cross-fitted metric. Twenty label-free \textbf{numeracy} features per text column (numbers, years, amounts,
percentages, length, character ratios) complete the input.

\paragraph{Cross-fitting.}
An expert fitted on $\mathcal{I}$ and applied to the same rows would look far better on training rows than on test
rows (observation (i)), and the learner would trust it too much. We split $\mathcal{I}$ into five folds
$\mathcal{I}_1,\dots,\mathcal{I}_5$ (stratified for classification) and define the expert feature of row $i$ as
\begin{equation}
  z_i = \hat g^{(-k)}(x_i) \;\text{ for } i \in \mathcal{I}_k, \qquad z_i = \hat g(x_i) \;\text{ for } i \notin \mathcal{I},
  \label{eq:crossfit}
\end{equation}
where $\hat g^{(-k)}$ is fitted on $\mathcal{I} \setminus \mathcal{I}_k$ and $\hat g$ on all of $\mathcal{I}$. Every
training-row feature thus comes from a model that did not see that row; the few selection choices (a Ridge penalty
from a small grid, the three depth layers, the top classes) are made once from all training rows' out-of-fold scores.
Validation and test rows only ever receive predictions, and the label-free steps (TF-IDF vocabulary, standardisation, PCA) are fitted on $\mathcal{I}$ too.
The learner gets, per text column, the PCA-30 components and the expert and numeracy columns, next to the structured
columns (TabM gets missing numeracy values filled with medians of $\mathcal{I}$, as the benchmark does). Each text is
encoded once; no encoder parameter is trained and all experts run on a CPU.

\paragraph{How we arrived here.}
Cross-fitting the fine-tuned TAR head addressed (i) but was no better than Joint TAR, at a high GPU cost; frozen
experts then matched Joint TAR, and the probes for (ii) and (iii) passed it.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We use the 20 MulTaBench text-tabular datasets (3 binary, 7 multiclass, 10 regression) and the benchmark's five folds
(test: 10\% of the rows, at most 2,000; training at most 10,000), scored with AUROC (macro one-vs-rest for multiclass)
or $R^2$ by the benchmark's code (tag \texttt{paper\_version}, identical to its 26 July 2026 release for these data). Baselines are the published Joint TAR and Joint Frozen scores of the same learner, dataset and fold;
TabPFN has none for two datasets ($n = 18$). Our re-implementation of Joint TAR scores \tarReproDiff{} against the
published numbers ($p = \tarReproP$), so our environment does not favour our method. Per learner, the fold-averaged
differences to Joint TAR are tested over datasets with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; each
test and its threshold ($p < 0.05$, positive mean) were fixed in writing before its run, and Holm corrects over all
\nTests{} pre-registered claims. All runs use seed 42, as the baselines.

\paragraph{Main result.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:results}a: with every learner our features beat Joint TAR, by \gainMin{} to

\paragraph{What each part contributes.}
Table~\ref{tab:buildup}. No expert alone beats Joint TAR; together they come close (\bucte), and the final-layer
probe (\buProbeAdd) and depth probes (\buDepthAdd) take the method to \budepth. Most of the gain thus comes from the
cross-fitted experts; the depth probes add only \buDepthAdd{} on their own (\depthIncWins{} of 20 datasets, $p =
\depthIncP$, not significant), so we read the depth profile mainly as an analysis of where the target sits. Cross-fitting is essential: the same
lexical expert fitted and applied in-sample scores \buLexInDrop{} lower, far below Joint TAR (observation (i)). Figure~\ref{fig:results}b: the best layer ranges from \depthMinLayer{} to
\depthMaxLayer{} and is an inner layer in \depthInner{} of 20 datasets.

\paragraph{Where it does not win.}
On Jigsaw Toxicity, where fine-tuning itself gains most (\jigsawTarGain{} over Joint Frozen) and no layer probe beats
the final one (\depthJigsaw), fine-tuning keeps a clear edge with every learner. Elsewhere our gain is unrelated to how much fine-tuning helps (Spearman $\rho = \rhoFineTune$).
On 36 further text datasets never used in development (CatBoost, folds 0--2) the gain carries over but is small: median \cSevenMedian, \cSevenWins{} of \cSevenN{} won, $p = \cSevenP$, not significant after
the Holm correction ($\cSevenHolm$).

\paragraph{Robustness and checks.}
The gain is not a lucky fold assignment: with two other cross-fitting seeds (LightGBM) it is \seedGainA{} and
\seedGainB{} (\seedWinsA{} of 20 datasets each, Holm $p = \seedHolm$), varies per dataset by a median \seedSd{} across
seeds and keeps its sign on \seedSameSign{} of 20.
Shuffled training labels drop the method to chance (AUROC \shuffleMin--\shuffleMax, $R^2$ \shuffleRtwo), and an
independent script reproduces Table~\ref{tab:main} from the raw results. The method saves the GPU, not time: our features take a median \costOurs{} minutes per run on a CPU (the frozen
encoding, done once per dataset, excluded), against \costTar{} for an optimised GPU fine-tuning of Joint TAR.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Cross-fitted predictions read from a frozen encoder beat fine-tuned text features with all five MulTaBench learners;
cross-fitting is what makes them safe to use. \textbf{Limitations.} Fine-tuning remains better for targets like toxicity that a frozen
encoder does not represent; gains on new datasets are smaller; and the method was developed on the 20 benchmark
datasets, early on with looks at their test scores, so they are no pristine holdout (later steps used
training-row development splits). \textbf{Future work.} Combining the experts
with fine-tuning where it helps; testing on new data such as VAERS-Serious, a vaccine adverse-event dataset (\url{https://github.com/DimaBsharaa/text-tabular-research-vaers}).
