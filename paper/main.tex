\begin{abstract}
Text-tabular learning combines structured attributes with free-text descriptions. MulTaBench's Joint TAR pipeline
fine-tunes the text encoder on each prediction task to produce target-aware embeddings. Can a frozen encoder support a
stronger alternative? We introduce Cross-Fitted Text Experts (CTE), which augments frozen E5 embeddings with
complementary predictions based on exact words, similar training examples, full embeddings, intermediate encoder
layers and attention over tokens, each trained out-of-fold, together with numerical features extracted from text. These features are combined
with the original structured columns and used by the benchmark's existing tabular learners. Across MulTaBench's 20
text-tabular datasets, CTE achieves higher mean scores than Joint TAR with all five learners, with improvements from
\gainMin{} to \gainMax{} AUROC~/~$R^2$, statistically significant for every learner (Wilcoxon test, $p < 0.01$).
Component comparisons demonstrate the benefit of combining different text signals, while layer analysis reveals that
predictive information is accessible at different encoder depths for different tasks. Our results show that combining
frozen representations with cross-fitted text experts provides an effective alternative to encoder fine-tuning on this
benchmark, although an earlier version's gains are smaller on 36 further datasets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Real-world tables often contain both structured attributes and free text. For example, a job posting may include
location and employment type alongside a detailed description, all used to predict whether the posting is fraudulent.
MulTaBench~\citep{arazi2026multabench} curates tasks where these sources provide complementary information, so that
combining them improves prediction over using either source alone, and benchmarks them with a modular pipeline: each text column
is embedded by e5-small-v2~\citep{wang2022e5}, compressed to 30 numbers with PCA, and placed next to the structured
columns for a tabular learner. In \emph{Joint Frozen} the encoder is used as published; in \emph{Joint TAR} its top
layers are first fine-tuned on the target with LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}, which makes the embeddings target-aware and
provides the main baseline for our evaluation. We investigate
whether target-awareness can instead be introduced through additional predictive features extracted from the text,
while retaining the benchmark's tabular learners.

Five considerations shape our design: (i)~in our pilot reimplementation of Joint TAR, the fine-tuned prediction head
scored an average of \leakGap{} higher on its own training rows than on test rows, with a positive gap in \leakPos{}
of \leakN{} runs across six datasets, motivating cross-fitted expert predictions; (ii)~PCA keeps the directions of
largest variance, not necessarily those that predict the target, motivating a probe on the full embeddings before
compression; (iii)~only the last encoder layer is read, although our training out-of-fold layer-probe analysis
identifies a layer other than the last as the most predictive in \depthInner{} of 20 datasets, motivating depth probes;
(iv)~sentence embeddings may not preserve all the predictive detail in specific words, character patterns, and
numerical amounts (``Riesling'', ``\$45''), motivating lexical and numeracy features and an attention probe over tokens; (v)~similar training examples
may provide useful local information about the target, motivating a neighbor expert. These are motivations, not
measured causes of Joint TAR's results.

We propose \emph{Cross-Fitted Text Experts} (CTE): the encoder stays frozen, and target-awareness enters as a few
compact columns, each a prediction of the target from one view of the text, with each expert fitted outside the
training fold it predicts. The tabular learner then fuses these columns with the structured ones, alongside the frozen
PCA features and label-free numeracy features. With all five MulTaBench learners, CTE achieves higher mean scores than
Joint TAR, significantly for each learner, on MulTaBench's 20 text-tabular datasets, and our analysis examines the contributions of the feature groups,
cross-fitting, and encoder depth. Code and results: \url{https://github.com/DimaBsharaa/text-tabular-nlp}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Text-tabular learning.} MulTaBench~\citep{arazi2026multabench} compares five learners on text- and
image-tabular datasets under unimodal, frozen and target-aware (TAR) conditions; TAR follows the representation tuning
of TabSTAR~\citep{arazi2025tabstar}, an end-to-end model that reads the text itself. We improve the modular pipeline
instead: our method retains the frozen e5-small-v2 encoder~\citep{wang2022e5}, while the Joint TAR baseline adapts it
using LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}. The learners are gradient-boosted trees (CatBoost~\citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost},
LightGBM~\citep{ke2017lightgbm}), a parameter-efficient MLP ensemble (TabM~\citep{gorishniy2025tabm}) and in-context
tabular foundation models (TabPFN-v2~\citep{hollmann2025tabpfnv2}, TabPFN-2.5~\citep{grinsztajn2025tabpfn25}).
\textbf{Stacking and cross-fitting.} Using held-out model predictions as features follows stacked
generalization~\citep{wolpert1992stacked,breiman1996stacked}. Our text experts serve as first-stage predictors whose
outputs become inputs to the tabular learner. Cross-fitting is also used to limit overfitting in double/debiased
machine learning~\citep{chernozhukov2018dml}.
\textbf{Probing.} Layer-wise probing has shown that different linguistic properties are accessible at different depths
in BERT~\citep{tenney2019bert}. We apply this idea to task-specific predictions from the layers of a frozen E5 encoder.

\paragraph{Setting.}
For one benchmark fold, let $\mathcal{I}$ be the learner's training rows ($|\mathcal{I}| \le 10{,}000$) and $f$ any
MulTaBench learner with its default settings. MulTaBench encodes each text cell as \texttt{"passage: <column>:
<value>"} with e5-small-v2, mean-pools and $\ell_2$-normalizes it into $e \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$, and reduces it to 30
dimensions by PCA fitted on $\mathcal{I}$. Our method augments this text-feature step (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}); the learner,
its settings, the splits and the metrics are the benchmark's own.

\paragraph{Experts.}
We keep Joint Frozen's features and add columns that each predict $y$ from the text, from linear, nearest-neighbor
or small attention models fitted on $\mathcal{I}$. The \textbf{lexical expert} (iv) concatenates a row's text columns
(\texttt{"col: value | \dots"}) and applies TF-IDF over word 1--2-grams and character 3--5-grams (50k
features each) followed by Ridge regression. The \textbf{neighbor expert} (v) averages the targets of up to 20 most similar training
rows (cosine similarity of the frozen embeddings, weights $1/(d+10^{-6})$ with $d$ the cosine distance), per text column and for the mean embedding
of all text columns (multiclass: the mean only). The \textbf{full-embedding probe} (ii) is a Ridge regression on the
concatenated full embeddings of all text columns, before PCA. The \textbf{depth probes} (iii) mean-pool each of the 12
layers, standardize them with statistics of $\mathcal{I}$, fit one Ridge probe per layer, and average the predictions
of the three layers with the best cross-fitted score on $\mathcal{I}$. The \textbf{attention probe} (iv) reads the
frozen token states (up to 256 per cell) of the best of these layers, standardized with statistics of $\mathcal{I}$:
per text column, additive attention~\citep{lin2017structured} with scores $v^\top\tanh(W h_t)$ (64 hidden units)
weights the tokens, and a linear layer maps the weighted sums of all columns to $y$ (AdamW, at most 40 epochs,
early-stopped on 10\% of each fit's training rows). The Ridge
experts regress one-hot classes or a rank-Gaussian transform of $y$ fitted within each expert fit (the attention probe
minimizes cross-entropy or squared error on the same targets); every expert appends one score (binary, regression), $C{-}1$ class scores (3--10 classes) or, for more
classes, the ten most frequent classes' scores plus the maximum normalized score and the entropy. Ridge penalties come from $\{0.3, 1, 3, 10\}$ ($\{1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300\}$ for the
standardized layers), chosen by the cross-fitted AUROC, macro one-vs-rest AUROC or Spearman correlation. Twenty
label-free \textbf{numeracy features} (iv) per text column (numbers, amounts, length, \dots) complete the input.

\paragraph{Cross-fitting} (i).
We split $\mathcal{I}$ into five folds $\mathcal{I}_1,\dots,\mathcal{I}_5$, stratified for classification when class
counts permit, and define the expert feature of row $i$ as
\begin{equation}
  z_i = \hat g^{(-k)}(x_i) \;\text{for } i \in \mathcal{I}_k, \qquad z_j = \hat g(x_j) \;\text{for } j \notin \mathcal{I}.
  \label{eq:crossfit}
\end{equation}
where $\hat g^{(-k)}$ is fitted on $\mathcal{I} \setminus \mathcal{I}_k$ and $\hat g$ on all of $\mathcal{I}$: training
rows receive predictions from expert fits excluding their fold, and validation and test rows from experts refitted on
all of $\mathcal{I}$ (the attention probe instead gives them the mean of its five fold fits). Ridge penalties and layer choices use training-wide out-of-fold scores, and frequent classes are
selected from training-label counts, so selection is training-wide rather than nested within each cross-fitting fold.
TF-IDF vocabulary, standardization and PCA use training inputs only; no validation or test label builds or selects
a feature.

\paragraph{How we arrived here.}
Cross-fitting the fine-tuned TAR head addressed (i) but was no better than Joint TAR, at a high computational cost;
frozen experts were better, and each probe above increased the mean gain further. Bringing the structured columns
into the text features did not help, possibly because the learner already combines them.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We use the 20 MulTaBench text-tabular datasets (3 binary, 7 multiclass, 10 regression), the benchmark's five folds
(test: 10\% of the rows, at most 2,000) and its code and metrics (AUROC, macro one-vs-rest for multiclass; $R^2$).
Baselines are the published Joint TAR and Joint Frozen scores of the same learner, dataset and fold; TabPFN has none
for two datasets ($n = 18$). Our re-implementation of Joint TAR scores \tarReproDiff{} against the
published numbers ($p = \tarReproP$), so our environment does not appear to favor us. Per learner, the fold-averaged
differences to Joint TAR are tested over datasets with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; each
test and its threshold ($p < 0.05$, positive mean) were fixed in writing before its run, and Holm corrects over all
\nTests{} pre-registered claims (Table~\ref{tab:tests}). The main runs use seed 42, as the baselines.

\paragraph{What each part contributes.}
Table~\ref{tab:buildup} (appendix): no expert alone beats Joint TAR; together they gain \bucte{} (not significant),
and the full-embedding, depth and attention probes add \buProbeAdd, \buDepthAdd{} and \buAttnAdd{} (to \buattn).
Cross-fitting is essential: the same lexical expert fitted and applied in-sample scores \buLexInDrop{} lower, far
below Joint TAR (observation (i)). The depth probes' own increment is not significant (\depthIncWins{} of 20
datasets, $p = \depthIncP$), so we read the depth profile (Figure~\ref{fig:results}b) mainly as an analysis: the best
layer ranges from \depthMinLayer{} to \depthMaxLayer{} and is a layer other than the last in \depthInner{} of 20
datasets.

\paragraph{The attention probe.}
Without it, Jigsaw Toxicity was the one dataset Joint TAR won with every learner (our gain \jigsawOldMin{} to
\jigsawOldMax): fine-tuning gains most there (\jigsawTarGain{} over Joint Frozen), no layer beats the last (at most
\depthJigsaw), and a single word can decide the label. Screened on training-row development splits, the
attention probe makes Jigsaw a win with every learner (\jigsawNewMin{} to \jigsawNewMax) and adds \attnIncPosMin{} to
\attnIncPosMax{} over the \emph{depth-probe version} (the method without it) for \attnIncN{} learners ($p \le
\attnIncPosPMax$). With LightGBM it is \attnIncLightGBM, all from Spotify Genres fold \spotifyFold, where
LightGBM scores \spotifyBad{} (other folds \spotifyOtherMin--\spotifyOtherMax; \spotifyAhundred{} when re-run on an A100 GPU).

\paragraph{Robustness and checks.}
Two checks used the depth-probe version. On 36 further text datasets never used in development (CatBoost, folds
0--2) the gain is small: median \cSevenMedian, \cSevenWins{} of \cSevenN{} won and \cSevenLosses{} lost
(\cSevenBigLosses{} by more than 0.07), $p = \cSevenP$, not significant after Holm ($\cSevenHolm$). With two other
cross-fitting seeds (LightGBM) the gain is \seedGainA{} and \seedGainB{} (\seedWinsA{} of 20 datasets each, Holm $p =
\seedHolm$ over the two seeds) and per dataset keeps its sign on \seedSameSign{} of 20. Shuffled training labels drop the final method to chance (AUROC \shuffleMin--\shuffleMax, $R^2 \le
\shuffleRtwo$); an independent script reproduces Table~\ref{tab:main} from the raw results.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\textbf{Can cross-fitted text experts outperform encoder fine-tuning?} On MulTaBench's 20 text-tabular datasets, yes:
predictions read from a frozen encoder beat Joint TAR with each of the five learners, and cross-fitting is what makes
them work.
\textbf{Limitations.} The method was developed on the 20 benchmark datasets, early on with looks at their test
scores, so they are no pristine holdout (later steps used training-row development splits); on 36 further datasets,
tested before the attention probe was added, the gain is smaller. \textbf{Future work.}
Combining the experts with fine-tuning; new data such as VAERS-Serious, vaccine adverse events
(\url{https://github.com/DimaBsharaa/text-tabular-research-vaers}).
