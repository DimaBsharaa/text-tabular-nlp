\begin{abstract}
Text-tabular learning combines structured attributes with free-text descriptions. MulTaBench's Joint TAR
pipeline fine-tunes the text encoder on each prediction task to produce target-aware embeddings. We ask
whether supervised experts can provide target-awareness while keeping the encoder frozen. Cross-Fitted
Text Experts (CTE) add a few compact columns, each a prediction of the target from one view of the text
(exact words, similar training rows, the full embedding, intermediate encoder layers or attention over
tokens), with predictions generated out-of-fold for training rows. The benchmark's tabular learners,
unchanged, receive these columns together with Joint Frozen's PCA-compressed embeddings, numerical features
extracted from the text and the original structured columns. Across MulTaBench's 20 text-tabular datasets,
CTE achieves higher mean scores than Joint TAR with all five learners, by \gainMin{} to \gainMax{}
AUROC~/~$R^2$, and scores higher in \runsWonPct\% of the \nRuns{} individual runs; the gains are
statistically significant for every learner in pre-registered Wilcoxon tests, Holm-corrected across the
five learners. Component comparisons support combining complementary text signals and using cross-fitted
predictions, and layer probes find a layer other than the last most predictive on \depthInner{} of 20
datasets. On this benchmark, cross-fitted text experts provide an effective alternative to encoder
fine-tuning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Real-world tables often contain both structured attributes and free text. For example, a job posting may include
location and employment type alongside a detailed description, all used to predict whether the posting is fraudulent.
MulTaBench~\citep{arazi2026multabench} curates tasks where these sources provide complementary information, so that
combining them improves prediction over using either source alone, and benchmarks them with a modular pipeline: each text column
is embedded by e5-small-v2~\citep{wang2022e5}, compressed to 30 numbers with PCA, and placed next to the structured
columns for a tabular learner. In \emph{Joint Frozen} the encoder is used as published; in \emph{Joint TAR} its top
layers are first fine-tuned on the target with LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}, which makes the embeddings target-aware and
provides the main baseline for our evaluation.

Five considerations shape our design: (i)~in our pilot reimplementation of Joint TAR, the fine-tuned prediction head
scored an average of \leakGap{} higher on its own training rows than on test rows, with a positive gap in \leakPos{}
of \leakN{} runs across six datasets, motivating cross-fitted expert predictions; (ii)~PCA keeps the directions of
largest variance, not necessarily those that predict the target, motivating a probe on the full embeddings before
compression; (iii)~only the last encoder layer is read, although our out-of-fold layer probes on training rows
identify a layer other than the last as the most predictive in \depthInner{} of 20 datasets, motivating depth probes;
(iv)~sentence embeddings may not preserve all the predictive detail in specific words, character patterns, and
numerical amounts (``Riesling'', ``\$45''), motivating lexical and numeracy features and an attention probe over tokens; (v)~similar training examples
may provide useful local information about the target, motivating a neighbor expert. These are motivations, not
measured causes of Joint TAR's results.

We propose Cross-Fitted Text Experts (CTE), which introduce target-awareness through supervised text predictions
while keeping the encoder frozen. Unchanged tabular learners combine these predictions with structured, PCA and
numeracy features. Across MulTaBench, CTE achieves higher mean scores than Joint TAR with all five
learners; we analyze feature contributions, cross-fitting and encoder depth. Code and results: \url{https://github.com/DimaBsharaa/text-tabular-nlp}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Text-tabular learning.} MulTaBench~\citep{arazi2026multabench} compares five learners on text- and
image-tabular datasets under unimodal, frozen and target-aware (TAR) conditions; TAR follows the representation tuning
of TabSTAR~\citep{arazi2025tabstar}, an end-to-end model that reads the text itself. We improve the modular pipeline
instead: our method retains the frozen e5-small-v2 encoder~\citep{wang2022e5}, while the Joint TAR baseline adapts it
using LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}. The learners are gradient-boosted trees (CatBoost~\citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost},
LightGBM~\citep{ke2017lightgbm}), a parameter-efficient MLP ensemble (TabM~\citep{gorishniy2025tabm}) and in-context
tabular foundation models (TabPFN-v2~\citep{hollmann2025tabpfnv2}, TabPFN-2.5~\citep{grinsztajn2025tabpfn25}).
\textbf{Stacking and cross-fitting.} Using held-out model predictions as features follows stacked
generalization~\citep{wolpert1992stacked,breiman1996stacked}. Our text experts serve as first-stage predictors whose
outputs become inputs to the tabular learner. Cross-fitting is also used to limit overfitting in double/debiased
machine learning~\citep{chernozhukov2018dml}.
\textbf{Probing.} Layer-wise probing has shown that different linguistic properties are accessible at different depths
in BERT~\citep{tenney2019bert}. We apply this idea to task-specific predictions from the layers of a frozen E5 encoder.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}


\paragraph{Setting.}
For one benchmark fold, let $\mathcal{I}$ be the learner's training rows ($|\mathcal{I}| \le 10{,}000$) and $f$ any
MulTaBench learner with its default settings. MulTaBench encodes each text cell as \texttt{"passage: <column>:
<value>"} with e5-small-v2, mean-pools and $\ell_2$-normalizes it into $e \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$, and reduces it to 30
dimensions by PCA fitted on $\mathcal{I}$. Our method augments this text-feature step (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}); the learner,
its settings, the splits and the metrics are the benchmark's own.

\paragraph{Experts.}
We keep Joint Frozen's features and add columns that each predict $y$ from the text, from linear, nearest-neighbor
or small attention models fitted on $\mathcal{I}$. The \textbf{lexical expert} (iv) concatenates a row's text columns
(\texttt{"col: value | \dots"}) and applies TF-IDF over word 1--2-grams and character 3--5-grams (50k
features each) followed by Ridge regression. The \textbf{neighbor expert} (v) averages the targets of up to 20 most similar training
rows (cosine similarity, weights $1/(d+10^{-6})$ with $d$ the cosine distance), per text column and for the mean embedding
of all text columns (multiclass: the mean only). The \textbf{full-embedding probe} (ii) is a Ridge regression on the
concatenated full embeddings of all text columns, before PCA. The \textbf{depth probes} (iii) mean-pool each of the 12
layers, standardize them with statistics of $\mathcal{I}$, fit one Ridge probe per layer, and average the predictions
of the three layers with the best cross-fitted score on $\mathcal{I}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:depth}b). The \textbf{attention probe} (iv) reads the
frozen token states (up to 256 per cell) of the best of these layers, standardized with statistics of $\mathcal{I}$:
per text column, additive attention~\citep{lin2017structured} with scores $v^\top\tanh(W h_t)$ (64 hidden units)
weights the tokens, and a linear layer maps the weighted sums of all columns to $y$ (AdamW, at most 40 epochs,
early-stopped on 10\% of each fit's training rows). The Ridge
experts regress one-hot classes or a rank-Gaussian transform of $y$ fitted within each expert fit (the attention probe
minimizes cross-entropy or squared error on the same targets); every expert appends one score (binary, regression), $C{-}1$ class scores (3--10 classes) or, for more
classes, the ten most frequent classes' scores plus the maximum normalized score and the entropy. Ridge penalties come from small grids ($\{0.3, \dots, 10\}$; $\{1, \dots, 300\}$ for the layers), chosen by
the cross-fitted AUROC or Spearman correlation. Twenty
label-free \textbf{numeracy features} (iv) per text column complete the input.


\paragraph{Cross-fitting (i).}
We split $\mathcal{I}$ into five folds $\mathcal{I}_1,\dots,\mathcal{I}_5$, stratified for classification when class
counts permit, and define the expert feature of row $i$ as
\begin{equation}
  z_i = \hat g^{(-k)}(x_i) \;\text{for } i \in \mathcal{I}_k, \qquad z_j = \hat g(x_j) \;\text{for } j \notin \mathcal{I},
  \label{eq:crossfit}
\end{equation}
where $\hat g^{(-k)}$ is fitted on $\mathcal{I} \setminus \mathcal{I}_k$ and $\hat g$ on all of $\mathcal{I}$: training
rows receive predictions from expert fits excluding their fold, and validation and test rows from experts refitted on
all of $\mathcal{I}$ (the attention probe instead gives them the mean of its five fold fits). Ridge penalties and layer choices use training-wide out-of-fold scores, and frequent classes are
selected from training-label counts, so selection is training-wide rather than nested within each cross-fitting fold.
TF-IDF vocabulary, standardization and PCA use training inputs only; no validation or test label builds or selects
a feature.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We use the 20 MulTaBench text-tabular datasets (3 binary, 7 multiclass, 10 regression), the benchmark's five folds
(test: 10\% of the rows, at most 2,000) and its code and metrics (AUROC; $R^2$).
Baselines are the published Joint TAR and Joint Frozen scores of the same learner, dataset, fold and seed (42); TabPFN cannot
run the two datasets with 30 and 114 classes ($n = 18$). Our re-implementation of Joint TAR scores \tarReproDiff{} against the
published numbers ($p = \tarReproP$), so our environment does not appear to favor us. Per learner, the fold-averaged
differences to Joint TAR are tested over datasets with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\citep{wilcoxon1945individual}; each
test and its threshold ($p < 0.05$, positive mean) were fixed in writing before its run. We Holm-correct~\citep{holm1979simple} over the
five learners; over all \nTests{} pre-registered comparisons (Tables~\ref{tab:tests}--\ref{tab:final_p}), \holmAllNote.

\paragraph{Main result.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:results}a: with every learner our features beat Joint TAR, by \gainMin{} to

\dsWonFour{} of the 20 datasets are won with at least four learners (\dsWonAll{} with every eligible learner; per dataset: Table~\ref{tab:per_dataset}). Gains are largest for
regression, followed by binary and multiclass classification (Figure~\ref{fig:results}b).

\paragraph{What each part contributes.}
Figure~\ref{fig:depth}a, Table~\ref{tab:buildup}: combined, the base experts gain \bucte{} over Joint TAR (not
significant) and the final configuration \buattn; none of the three base experts alone is ahead. Fitted in-sample, the lexical expert scores \buLexInDrop{} lower. The
depth increment alone is not significant (\depthIncWins{} of 20 datasets, $p = \depthIncP$), so Figure~\ref{fig:depth}b is
mainly an analysis.

\paragraph{The attention probe.}
Without it, Jigsaw Toxicity was the one dataset Joint TAR won with every learner, and a single word can decide its label. Screened on training-row development splits, the
attention probe turns Jigsaw into a win with every learner on the benchmark folds (\jigsawNewMin{} to \jigsawNewMax) and adds \attnIncPosMin{} to
\attnIncPosMax{} over the \emph{depth-probe version} (the method without it) \attnIncNote.

\paragraph{Robustness and checks.}
Two checks used the depth-probe version. On 36 further text datasets never used in development (CatBoost, folds
0--2) the gain over Joint TAR is small: median \cSevenMedian, \cSevenWins{} of \cSevenN{} won and \cSevenLosses{} lost
(\cSevenBigLosses{} by more than 0.07), $p = \cSevenP$, not significant after Holm ($\cSevenHolm$). With two other
cross-fitting seeds (LightGBM) the gain is \seedGainA{} and \seedGainB{} (\seedWinsA{} of 20 datasets each, Holm $p =
\seedHolm$ over the two seeds). Label-shuffle checks of the final method give chance-level performance, as expected (AUROC
\shuffleMin--\shuffleMax, $R^2 \le \shuffleRtwo$).

\section{Conclusion and summary}
\label{sec:conclusion}
CTE combines cross-fitted text predictions with structured features while keeping the encoder frozen and the tabular
learners unchanged. It achieves higher mean scores than Joint TAR with all five learners. Component comparisons support
combining complementary text signals, while layer analysis identifies predictive information beyond the final encoder layer.

\textbf{Limitations.} Benchmark test scores informed development; an earlier version showed smaller gains on 36
additional datasets. Future work will combine these experts with encoder fine-tuning.

\textbf{Additional Track 2 contribution.} We also curated VAERS-Serious, which satisfies both joint-signal and
task-awareness conditions with all five learners under the official MulTaBench pipeline. Its dataset,
preprocessing, notebooks and results are available in the
\href{https://github.com/DimaBsharaa/text-tabular-research-vaers}{\textcolor{blue!60!black}{accompanying repository}},
provided for consideration as additional project work.
